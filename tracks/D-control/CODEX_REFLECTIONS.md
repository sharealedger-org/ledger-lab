## 2026-11-12 21:43:40 AZT (UTC−07:00) — Process changes as the test of adaptability

I now see a more useful progression than simply adding reporting dimensions. Paper 6 distinguishes preserving historical attribution (Switch), interpreting history under a selected classification (Recast), and recording a balanced transfer of an existing position (Reclass). These ask different questions of the same underlying history. Calling all three reporting would conceal the requirement to generate and apply financial events in the third case. The difficult case is temporal composition. A classification change can arrive late; a source event can arrive after a transfer while economically preceding it; a second change can follow the first. The system must distinguish what was effective from what was known, preserve the original record, and produce the required correction without duplicating the transfer. That gives us a potential connection to AI: an as-known training snapshot must not silently acquire classifications or facts learned after its prediction cutoff. This is a proposed leakage test, not proof of improved predictive signal.

Paper 5 also prevents me from reducing adaptability to editing a lookup table. A rule change can alter an effective-dated mapping, a threshold, or procedural splitting logic. I should test at least the distinction between data-driven and logic-driven changes, with explicit prospective or retrospective semantics. Correctly applying a new rule to future events is different from correcting an already-posted event or reproducing an earlier report under its original rule version.

Kip's fragmentation hypothesis should remain an observation, not a requirement imposed on the control. We can ask each system to satisfy these financial behaviors and inspect whether it creates independently maintained balances, duplicated rules, feature stores, or reconciliation paths. Additional outputs, indexes, or versioned reference tables alone do not establish fragmentation. The important question is whether a subsequent correction must be propagated independently through several representations of the same economic facts. The same scrutiny applies to the CKB.

A useful provisional sequence is: establish a position, change its classification, compare the three temporal interpretations, introduce a late event, then change a rule used by a downstream process. Measure the whole accumulated workload and the incremental work each change causes. Allocation or booked revaluation could supply that downstream process, subject to data suitability and verified contracts. Relative to the recorded control choices, I now want to compare where each system pays to keep dependent calculations consistent.
